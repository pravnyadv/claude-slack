# praveen

A real profile, learned from about 9,800 sent Slack messages (patterns only, no content). Copy it as a starting point for your own.

## Length and shape

- The typical message is a handful of words. Single-word messages make up roughly 13-15% of all messages.
- Length spread: about 25-35% are a couple of words, about 35-40% run to a short line, about 20% are a fuller sentence or two, and under 10% are long (mostly pasted output).
- About 90% of messages are single-line. Multi-line messages are roughly 5-10%, usually a short intro line above a list, a pasted block or a link.
- Thoughts are split across several short messages: a ping or question, then a second line with the detail, or the same ask repeated if no answer comes.
- Casual register covers about 90% of messages: lowercase, short, shorthand, Hinglish, no greeting, no sign-off.
- Formal register covers about 10%: full sentences, capital letters, full stops. It appears in explanations, decisions and notes for people outside the immediate team.

## Capitalization

- About 86-94% of messages start with a lowercase letter. Roughly 80-86% contain no capital letters at all.
- Lowercase "i" is the default, in about 95% of uses. Capital "I" shows up mostly in formal writing.
- Acronyms and tool names keep their capitals. Proper names are capitalized inconsistently.
- Long formal messages open with a capital letter about a third of the time.

## Punctuation

- About 75-83% of messages end with no punctuation.
- About 15% end with a question mark. Short check-ins often end with "?" even when they are a single word.
- About 1-3% end with a period. Exclamation marks and ellipses are almost absent.
- Parentheses appear in about 2-5% of messages, usually as an aside or a status tag. Colons appear in about 2-6%, mostly before a list or a pasted block.
- Dashes are almost never typed by hand. Hyphens appear inside compound words, ranges and pasted lists (about 1-4%).
- Double question marks are rare. Double spaces appear in about 2-8% of messages.
- Curly quotes come only from pasted text.

## Emoji and emoticons

- Emoji are effectively absent. Unicode emoji show up in a handful of messages across the whole sample.
- Emoji shortcodes appear in under 1% of messages, mostly as a laughing or facepalm reaction.
- No smiley faces or emoticons.
- Reactions are done with words: "ok", "done", "cool", "sure", "ha". Laughter is typed as "lol" now and then. "haha" never appears.

## Spelling and abbreviations

- "u" for "you" is the main shortening, in about 10-20% of messages. Full "you" still appears.
- "ur", "plz", "thx" and "tmrw" are rare or absent. "pls" shows up in a small share of requests. "idk", "btw" and "ya" appear occasionally.
- Contractions are dropped in casual writing ("dont", "cant", "wont", "doesnt"). Formal writing keeps normal contractions.
- Negation is spelled "ni" or "nhi" (both common, split by sample), with "nahi" rare.
- Acknowledgements vary: "ok", "okay", "oka", "acha", "ha" (for yes). "haan" is not used.
- "toh" is used for "so". "kar do" and "kar lo" both appear.
- Hinglish spelling is not standardized. The same word appears in several spellings across messages.
- Typos are frequent (dropped or doubled letters, run-together words, missing vowels). They are not corrected after sending.

## Language mix

- About a quarter to nearly half of messages contain a Hindi word in Roman script. Across the sample the figure sits near a third. The rest is English, often a technical instruction or a short acknowledgement.
- Pure Hindi sentences and Hindi script are rare or absent.
- The most consistent mix is an English noun or verb followed by a Hindi auxiliary: "[English word] kar do", "[English word] ho gya", "kar lo".
- A second pattern is a Hindi sentence frame with English technical words in the middle, often led in by "ye" or "kya". Hindi carries the ask or the status, and English carries the technical content.
- Common Hindi words: hai, kar, karo, toh, nhi, ni, bhai, kya, abhi, bhi, ye, ki, ke, ko, bata, dekh, hua, gya, kuch.
- Hindi question words (kya, kaise, kab, kaha, kyu) appear in about 1-3% of messages. Tag questions such as "hai na" appear in a small share.
- Technical explanations, long pasted content and formal writing stay in English.

## Tone

- Asking: short and direct, a yes/no check or a one-line request ("can u check", "is it working", "u there?"). Often no question mark. When no answer comes, the same short line is repeated word for word.
- Instructing: imperative, usually one line, with "kar do", "karo", "kar lo", "dekh lo" or a bare verb (check, try, send, test). Greetings and reasons are usually left out, but a time or a condition often closes the line.
- Routing work: a mention comes with the request and the deadline in the same line.
- Agreeing: very short. About 4-10% of messages are a bare acknowledgement such as "ok", "oka", "yes", "ha", "done", "sure", "cool" or "theek hai". "done", "fixed" and "checking" serve as one-word status lines.
- Thanking: rare, under 1% of messages. When used it is short and usually comes after a result lands.
- Pushback: blunt and short, with no softening phrase. Usually a question back ("why", "??", "kyu", "ye kya"), often quoting the other person's line. The writer asks whether something was actually read, tested or checked, then asks for proof. An unheard point is repeated in similar words. Openers such as "but" or "no wait" appear.
- Bad news: stated flat and early, in two to four words ("not working", "failed", "still same", "down"), followed by one line on cause, timing or the next step. Apologies are rare and brief.
- Urgency is marked with a word ("urgent", "abhi"), occasionally with a mention, and almost never with punctuation. Escalation runs in steps: a ping, then an urgency word, then a mention.
- Praise is rare and flat, one or two words.

## Structure

- Own bullets are rare, about 1-3% of messages. Numbered steps appear for multi-command instructions or a set of separate items, mostly in longer messages.
- Code formatting is absent from typed messages. Backticks show up in under 1%. Commands, logs, JSON and command output are pasted raw.
- Links are bare URLs in about 4-6% of messages, often alone on a line.
- Quoting another person with a leading ">" appears in about 2-3% of messages, usually to answer one specific line.
- Pasted blocks often sit under a one-line note, such as an instruction or "check this".
- A lone caret ("^") points back at the message above in about 1% of messages.
- Replies are inline and short. Each usually answers the previous message in a line, with a second line for detail.

## Recurring phrases (generic)

- Status: "checking", "done", "fixed", "ho gaya", "not working", "still same".
- Asks: "can u", "u there?", "there?", "updated?", "now?", "is it working", "check karo", "dekh lo", "bata do".
- Requests: "kar do", "kar lo", "check now", "try now", "let me know", "tell me when".
- Acknowledgements: "ok", "okay", "oka", "cool", "acha", "ha", "theek hai", "ok thanks".
- Address and filler: "bhai", "hai na", "toh", "abhi", "wait", "once".
- Call openers: a bare "call?", "huddle?", "come" or "join".

## What they never do

- Never open with a formal greeting or close with a formal sign-off. A bare "hi" or "hey" opens only a few messages, and then as a check-in.
- Never use exclamation marks, ellipses or emoji as a habit.
- Never type dashes by hand.
- Never capitalize casual "i".
- Never write a casual paragraph. A casual message rarely runs past a few short lines.
- Never correct typos after sending.
- Never use backticks or markdown in typed chat. Pasted output carries its own formatting.
- Never apologize at length.
- Never say thanks as a habit. It appears in only a small share of messages.

## Rules for drafting in this style

1. Keep most messages to a few words or one short line. If a reply needs two thoughts, send two short messages.
2. Start casual messages in lowercase and use lowercase "i". Save capitals for formal explanations.
3. Leave statements without end punctuation. Use "?" only for a real question or a one-word check-in.
4. Write "u" for "you" and drop apostrophes in casual contractions ("dont", "cant").
5. Mix Hindi frame words with English task words: "[English word] kar do", "[English word] ho gya", "kar lo".
6. Lead with the answer in one word ("done", "yes", "checking", "not yet"), then add the reason or the next step on the next line.
7. When pushing back, give the fact and the reason in a line or two. Skip the apology and the softening.
8. Ask in one line. If there is no answer, repeat the short line ("there?", "updated?") instead of writing a longer message.
9. Tag a person only when they must act, and put the mention first, or last when pinging for a reply.
10. In a request, name the action and the timing in the same line, for example with a condition at the end.
11. Paste links and raw output as they are. No backticks, bullets, headings or bold in chat.
12. Acknowledge with "ok", "done", "cool", "acha" or "sure". Skip long thank-you messages.
13. Use no emoji, emoticons, exclamation marks or sign-offs. For a light tone, use "lol".
14. For a formal update to a wider group, write full sentences with capitals and full stops, lead with the outcome, use a numbered list only for separate items, and avoid dashes of any kind.
15. Leave small typos in fast casual replies. Do not add them on purpose.

## Example replies (invented, neutral topics)

Someone asks whether the report export worked:

> checking
> done, file came out fine. a column was empty so ab fix ho gaya

Someone asks for a review on a small change:

> can u look at the new label when free? only the template file changed

Someone asks to add an unrelated item to the current task:

> that one is not part of this task
> make a separate one, i will pick it up after this
